The inspection schedule

Both codes set the minimum interval for inspecting the whole exhaust system for grease buildup. Inspection results decide whether it gets cleaned.

NFPA 96 says inspections must be done by a “properly trained, qualified, and certified person(s) acceptable to the authority having jurisdiction.” The IFC lets the fire code official approve other intervals.

When does a hood have to be cleaned?

When the inspection finds grease. Under the IFC, if hoods, grease-removal devices, fans, ducts or other parts have an accumulation of grease, they “shall be cleaned in accordance with ANSI/IKECA C10” (Section 607.3.3.2). NFPA 96 gives a measurable trigger: place a grease depth gauge comb on the surface, and clean when it reads 0.078 in. (2,000 µm) on a surface or 0.125 in. (3,175 µm) in a fan housing.

Source for the depth limits: NFPA 96 first-revision report for the 2026 edition (grease depth text), which shows the current text alongside the proposed renumbering. Section titles for the 2024 edition are in the NFPA 96, 2024 edition, free preview (table of contents).

Records and tags the fire code asks for

Records (IFC 2021, 607.3.3.3)

After every inspection, record the individual and company that did it, a description of the inspection, and when it happened. After every cleaning, record the individual and company and when it happened. Records must be completed after each visit and kept.

Tags (IFC 2021, 607.3.3.3.1)

When the hood or duct system is inspected, leave a tag in a conspicuous location with the service provider’s name, address, phone number and date of service. Cover or remove older tags.

Reports to the owner

Several jurisdictions go further. Utah requires a completion and deficiency report for the owner, and Portland requires the label to list any areas not cleaned and the owner to keep reports on site.

FAQ

How often does a restaurant hood have to be cleaned?

The codes schedule inspections, and cleaning happens when an inspection finds grease buildup. NFPA 96 (2024 edition, Table 12.4) sets the inspection schedule at monthly for solid fuel cooking, quarterly for high-volume cooking (24-hour, charbroiling, wok), semiannually for moderate volume, and annually for low volume (churches, day camps, seasonal businesses, senior centers). The International Fire Code uses the same intervals. In practice, a busy kitchen ends up cleaned on roughly that schedule.

How much grease is too much?

NFPA 96 uses a depth gauge comb. Surfaces with 0.078 in. (2,000 µm) of grease, or a fan housing with 0.125 in. (3,175 µm), must be cleaned. That’s the wording in NFPA’s published revision report for the next edition, linked below.

Who decides which category my kitchen is in?

Your Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), usually the local fire marshal. The IFC lets the fire code official approve a different interval, and some cities, like Portland, publish their own policy. Ask your fire department if you’re unsure.

What has to go on the hood sticker?

Under IFC 2021 Section 607.3.3.3.1, the tag must show the service provider’s name, address, phone number and the date of service, in a conspicuous spot, with older tags covered or removed. Some states prescribe the label in detail. Utah, for example, specifies its size, color and contents.

Do I need a license to clean hoods?

It depends on where you work. Massachusetts, Utah and Nevada require state licensing, and Portland, Oregon requires a city certificate. Many states have no hood-specific license, and the work is governed by the local fire code instead. Check with your state fire marshal and your city.

Is this legal advice?

No. It’s a plain-English summary of NFPA 96, the International Fire Code and the official pages linked here, checked October 17, 2026. Your jurisdiction may adopt a different edition or add rules, and the official text is what counts.
