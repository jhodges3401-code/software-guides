How to start a pest control business (2026)

Short answer

In all 12 states we checked, you need a business license for each location and a certified person who qualifies it by passing the state’s exams. Most require 1 to 3 years of experience. Then insure to the state’s minimums, set up application records, and register before you advertise.

1. Two licenses, not one

Almost every state licenses two things separately:

  • The business gets a license, registration or charter for each location. In several states (Texas is one example) you can’t advertise or solicit until it’s issued.
  • A certified person (certified operator, qualifying manager, qualifying party or responsible certified applicator, depending on the state) is attached to that business and passes the exams. Technicians who do the spraying usually register under that person.

That’s why the experience rule matters most. You can form the company on day one, but in most states it can’t work until someone with enough documented field time has passed the exams and is named on the license.

Underneath the state rules sits the federal baseline: anyone who uses or supervises restricted-use pesticides must be a certified applicator under a state plan (40 CFR Part 171). The states go further and cover general-use structural work too.

2–3 and 5. Experience, exams, then business registration

The typical path looks like this (exact names and hours differ by state):

  1. Work as a registered technician or apprentice under a licensed company while you log the documented experience your state requires for the person who will qualify a business.
  2. Complete any pre-exam courses the agency names (Texas, for example, lists classroom and on-the-job hours before a technician license; California requires board-approved pre-operator courses within 3 years of the exam). Use the agency’s own licensing page for course lists and current prices—we don’t invent training fees here.
  3. Pass the core exam plus each category you’ll sell (household pest, wood-destroying organisms, lawn and ornamental, fumigation, and so on). Florida, for instance, certifies the operator in charge in every category the location works in.
  4. Buy insurance (and a bond where required) on the state’s form, at or above its minimums.
  5. Apply for the business license for each location, naming the certified person. Register technicians and solicitors where the state requires it. Get a local city or county business license too.
  6. Only then advertise. Several agencies say solicitation before the business license issues is not allowed.

Experience examples already sourced on the license guide: 3 years as a service employee in Florida (with a degree shortcut), 2 years field experience for a California Branch 2 Operator (including one year as a Field Representative), 2 years in Georgia and North Carolina, 12 months of verifiable experience in Texas for the responsible certified applicator. Full cards and official links: license requirements by state.

Listed state fees on that same page (checked October 17, 2026) put business licenses roughly from $35/year (Ohio, Pennsylvania) to $300 (Texas, Florida) or $900 for a roughly 3-year New York registration, with exams from $10 to $300 each. Exam fees paid to a testing vendor (Texas uses Metro after TDA pre-approval) are separate—confirm on the agency site.

6. Application records from day one

Once you’re working, every state on our list expects a record for each application—usually kept 2 or 3 years. Typical fields: date and times, customer and site, target pest, product name, EPA registration number, amount or rate, method, and the applicator’s license number.

Our pesticide application records by state guide has each state’s retention rule, the official source link, and a free printable log template. Set the form (or software fields) up before the first job, not after.

FAQ

Do I need a license to start a pest control business?

In every state we checked, yes. Each one requires a business license, registration or charter for the company, and a certified or licensed person who qualifies it. Who that person must be, and how much experience they need, is what varies most. See the 12-state license guide for the details.

Can I start with no experience?

Usually not as the person who qualifies the company. Most of the 12 states require one to three years of documented experience for that person (for example 3 years in Florida, 2 in California, Georgia and North Carolina), though a related degree can shorten it in several states. A common path is to work as a registered technician first, or to hire a certified operator to qualify your company while you get your own hours.

How much do the state license fees cost?

The listed government fees themselves are modest. On our license guide, business licenses run from $35 a year (Ohio, Pennsylvania) to $300 (Texas, Florida) or $900 for a roughly 3-year New York registration, and exams run from $10 to $300 each—all as published by those agencies, checked October 17, 2026. Insurance, bonds, training courses and your time cost far more. We don’t invent course or exam-vendor prices here; use each agency’s own fee page.

What insurance do I need?

Whatever your state lists as the minimum, on its own certificate form, before the business license issues. Examples from states we already documented: Texas $500,000 / $1,000,000 on TDA form ALS-1101; New York $1,000,000 per occurrence with NYSDEC as certificate holder; California $500,000 general liability plus a $12,500 surety bond. Full sourced figures are on the license-by-state page.
